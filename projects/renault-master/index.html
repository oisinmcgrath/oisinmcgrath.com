Where it started

On June 21st 2025, bright as a new penny, I embarked on what became both a fascinating and a multilayered project.

Once a post van, 'Big Red' as I soon affectionately named it was en route for a new life on the vast stretches of remote Australian highway. Now the real work begins.

After weeks of tempering my frustration and disappointment participating in the auctions, endurance satiated my ambition when I became the proud owner of a 2016 ex Australia Post Renault Master, having just clocked over 200,000 kms.

I captured this proud moment, of my first time driving the newly acquired post van, turning from its old life onto a new frontier. I was grinning like a boy again, ready to take on my first independent project of this scale.

Getting started

Until I started, I didn't realise that every design decision unavoidably affects every other part of the build as it progresses. Every system I intended to implement was essential — and so was everything each of them demanded — and all of it had to work around the rest inside a few cubic metres.

There was wiring to consider, the order of operations — that was a big one — what should be done first, and what should be done last?

Since I was going to be driving this van throughout the project, I decided that dealing with the road noise would be a gratifying and low-stakes opportunity to get the ball rolling. It was at this stage that ChatGPT and later Claude found their place in the toolkit, after proving their worth researching products, materials and methods.

The first fascinating product I discovered was butyl sound-deadening mats. Like a dense sticky sheet of chewing gum, you apply these adhesive squares to the centre of any of the noisy steel panels inside the vehicle which reverberate while driving, which means the driving experience goes from a noisy rattle to smooth and elegant.

I got to work applying the mats, rationing the supply I had purchased. Knowing the rear wheels were a major source of noise, I prioritised scribing the mats to the shape of the rear wheel arches, managing to cover about 85% of the arches. For the same reason I built plywood boxes over the arches, acting as an auditory buffer and giving me a container for more insulation, Earthwool batts.

Ready to begin my work experience as a courier, I set out in my now quiet van to begin the scavenge for the build materials.

An electrical system is one of those items that requires forethought: I knew the installation of a house battery, separate from the vehicle's own battery, would be core to this build, so I didn't hesitate to pick up the necessary components.

This project taught me the importance of planning, and as such I regret none of the decisions I made during the build.

My strategy throughout the build was to pick the most reversible next step, and then stand back to visualise the next direction. Given the project was a complex undertaking, I wanted to avoid inertia; I knew the right design would reveal itself during the process.

Building first with pencil and paper is fine, if you know the necessary considerations of the project. This being my first build, I felt it was my only available method. It wasn't that I didn't plan, but rather I found that planning at the required granularity proved futile: one consideration I wasn't aware of voided that time-expensive plan.

The van

Vehicle2016 Renault Master, medium wheelbase, high roof (L2H2) panel van
Engine2.3 litre twin-turbo diesel
TransmissionSix speed automatic
Weight3,510 kg gross — drivable on a normal car licence
Standing height1.85 m inside
DistanceAbout 220,000 km at the point of sale

Fit-out

Painted plywood cabinetry, European beech benchtops, and a tongue-and-groove timber ceiling. A door was fitted between the cab and the living area.

Sleeping and seating

A fixed double bed on a frame across the back, with a sprung mattress, so it needs no setting up. The whole space under it is open storage reachable from the rear doors, and there is a second storage area over the cab. The seating below the bed has a table that pulls out and stows.

Kitchen and water

A stainless sink plumbed to a 40 litre fresh water tank, with a pull-out sprayer tap that reaches out the side door to wash at. A 60 litre compressor fridge, and a chemical toilet with its own 10 litre fresh and 15 litre waste tanks.

Power

A 100 amp-hour battery in the back, charged off the engine's alternator through a voltage-sensitive relay so it only draws once the starter battery is full. A 300 watt inverter on an Anderson plug for mains sockets, and USB outlets through the living area. I ran 8 gauge UV-rated cable to the roof through sealed glands so panels could be added later without opening anything up again.

Heating and ventilation

A 5 kilowatt diesel heater plumbed off the van's own fuel, and a ten-speed roof fan with a thermostat and remote that can pull air in or push it out.

In testing, the heater proved more than adequate for the space. The fan became essential travelling the north of the country — it really earned its stripes up there.

Safety

Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and a fire extinguisher mounted by the door.

Making it road legal

The van came with a single seat. I fitted a passenger seat and belt, had the fitment inspected, and it was licensed as a two-seater under a Western Australian Department of Transport modification approval permit, which passes to the next owner with the vehicle.

Testing it

I ran three long-distance trips in it, the longest a 4,000 kilometre return drive to Darwin. Testing the van for reliability was a big reason for going that far.

That trip found a fault. The clutch runs off a concentric slave cylinder inside the bellhousing, so reaching it means dropping the gearbox out — not a roadside job, and the reason the part is a known weak point on these vans. I had it done in Katherine, in the Northern Territory, with the parts freighted up to Darwin: gearbox out, slave cylinder replaced, clutch bled, fault codes cleared, and a test drive afterwards, in November 2025.

Afterwards

It sold, and I hope the new owner is getting good use out of it. I'll never forget what I learned building it, and I hope you enjoyed me sharing it here.
